## The Structural Condition: Accountability Outgrew the Project Model

For most of the last three decades, multilateral development finance was organized around discrete projects. An implementing agency signed a loan agreement, executed procurement, delivered an asset — a road, a health facility, a training program, a digital system — and submitted a closeout report. Monitoring and evaluation happened at the edges. A baseline study at the start, a mid-term review, an impact evaluation at the end. The evidence cycle ran on a calendar that had little to do with how the program actually operated.

That model still exists. It is still how the majority of projects in the IDB, World Bank, CAF, and bilateral aid portfolios close. But something is shifting in how funders design new programs, particularly in social protection, public safety, environmental governance, and climate-adjacent infrastructure. The monitoring cycle is compressing. The evidence burden is moving closer to real-time. And the platform that produces that evidence is no longer an accessory to the project. In a growing number of programs, the platform is the project.

This changes what governments and implementers need to procure, in what order, and why.

## What the Accountability Shift Actually Looks Like

Three forces are moving the center of gravity.

The first is the Sustainable Development Goals framework itself. The SDGs require indicators that are geographically disaggregated and time-resolved. A national literacy rate reported every five years does not answer questions about which parishes are off-track now. A child poverty indicator aggregated to the country level does not tell a social protection ministry which districts need a referral backbone this quarter. The indicator standards are moving faster than the legacy data infrastructure that was designed to feed them.

The second is the climate finance reporting environment. The Green Climate Fund, the Loss and Damage Fund, and IFC's climate-linked facilities now require monitoring, reporting, and verification data that cannot be produced by retrospective surveys. Methane emissions. Carbon sequestration in verified projects. Adaptation outcomes tied to observed climate events. All of it requires instrumentation that operates continuously, not episodically. The funders have started asking for the MRV architecture up front, and in some cases funding it directly.

The third is the quiet institutional pressure from fiduciary trustees and board-level auditors. When a multilateral institution commits public capital from member states, it needs to show that the money moved outcomes, not just outputs. "We built the clinic" is an output. "Adolescent health service continuity in the target parish improved by X percent" is an outcome. Producing the second reliably requires data flows that the old project structure rarely generated.

The practical consequence is that a growing set of programs are now designed with three layers instead of two: the intervention itself, the operational capability to deliver it, and the intelligence infrastructure that produces evidence continuously from both. The intelligence infrastructure is no longer an evaluation line item at the end of the logframe. It is a foundation component, funded early, and it has to exist before the intervention can be credibly measured.

## Why the Platform Moves Before the Program

When programs were designed around a single built asset, sequencing was straightforward. Design the asset, procure it, deliver it, evaluate it. When programs are designed around continuous outcomes, the sequence inverts.

The intelligence layer has to come first, because without it there is no baseline to measure against. Case management, referral tracking, aggregate indicator scoring, and beneficiary flows all produce the evidence that the subsequent intervention will be judged on. If that layer shows up late, the program spends its first year scrambling to retroactively construct an evidence base from fragmented sources. Many programs never fully recover from that sequencing error.

This is where development finance starts to look less like project finance and more like operational infrastructure finance. The question is no longer "how much does the asset cost." The question is closer to "what does the operating system for this program cost, and over what horizon does it run." Multi-year operating commitments. A platform that produces indicators every week. Staff that operate it. Integrations with the sector systems that already exist (education rolls, health records, justice system data, environmental monitoring networks). The platform is the spine. Everything else attaches to it.

For funders, this is actually a better accountability model. A platform generates evidence as a byproduct of being used. The same system that routes a referral produces the referral-rate indicator. The same system that tracks an environmental intervention produces the change-detection evidence. The work and the evidence stop being separate activities.

## What This Changes for Program Designers

For governments and implementing agencies working with multilateral capital in 2026, three practical implications follow.

The first is that the procurement strategy needs to put governance and data foundations ahead of sector-specific tools. A youth protection program needs the data governance layer before it needs the case management workflow. An environmental program needs the operational data spine before it needs the domain dashboards. Sequencing this backwards creates expensive rework — the tools get procured, and then six months later someone realizes the data flows underneath them are fragmented, ungoverned, and unreportable. The remediation costs often exceed what it would have taken to do it in the correct order.

The second is that the institutional arrangements need to be designed for continuous evidence flow, not periodic reporting. This is where many well-funded programs have struggled. The data exists in ministries. The institutions are willing to share. But the cross-agency governance framework that allows continuous flow — role-based access, dynamic masking of personal data, entity resolution, auditable lineage — takes deliberate design and political work. When this framework is treated as an afterthought, the program ends up with a dashboard that updates annually because the pipelines behind it are manual.

The third is that the platform has to deliver value to the participating institutions independent of the funded program. This is a quiet requirement that funders are increasingly explicit about. If the intelligence platform only matters to the IDB-funded program, it will atrophy when the program closes. If the same platform also gives the Ministry of Education a better view of its own enrollment data, and the Ministry of Health a better view of its own adolescent indicators, the institutions have their own reasons to keep it operating. Sustainability stops being a fight after year five. It becomes a natural outcome because the platform was useful to each institution from the beginning.

## The Emerging Procurement Pattern

A shape is starting to emerge in how sophisticated governments are structuring these programs. It usually looks like this.

A small proof-of-value phase funded by a grant instrument (a facility like the Galápagos Life Fund, a preparation grant from IDB-Lab, a pilot window from a bilateral donor). The deliverable is a working platform at small scale with real data, real workflows, and a real institutional user. The point of the phase is to prove the design — and to give the multilateral funder the confidence to approve a larger, multi-year operational commitment.

Then a multi-year operational commitment, funded through the main loan or grant instrument, that covers the platform's steady-state cost, the sector-specific interventions that sit on top of it, and the measurement framework that reports back to the funder. This is where the program lives. The evidence flows continuously. The funder sees real indicators on a quarterly cadence, not an annual one.

And finally a transition plan — from day one — that describes how the platform will be absorbed into the recurring budgets of the participating institutions when the external financing ends. This is the part that most programs used to skip and that the better-designed 2026 programs explicitly address.

For the implementers, the integrators, and the technical operators who build and run these systems, the message is straightforward. Development finance has started buying a different kind of thing. It is worth designing programs that meet it where it is.
