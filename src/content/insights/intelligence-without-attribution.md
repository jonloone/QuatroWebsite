## The structural condition: the lesson is paid for but never delivered

An operator loses product to organized theft. In the weeks that follow, the operation learns a great deal: which stretch of perimeter was chosen and why, what time window the crew preferred, how the goods moved after they left, and — often — where they were sold. This knowledge is expensive. It was purchased with lost inventory, damaged equipment, and sometimes risk to people.

Then the knowledge stops moving. It stays inside one company's incident reports, because the operator has run the arithmetic on sharing it. Reporting the loss to an industry group identifies the operation as a target that was successfully hit. Insurers may reprice. Buyers may quietly ask about security of supply. Competitors may read weakness. And the adversary — who often has better lateral visibility across the sector than any single victim — learns what was detected and what was not.

So each operator in the sector faces the same organized networks alone, learns the same lessons independently, and pays full price for intelligence a neighbor already owns. The adversary, meanwhile, enjoys the one structural advantage no defender has: they see the whole sector, because they work across it.

## Why the obvious fix fails

The instinctive answer is an information-sharing group — a sector association, a working committee, a group chat of security managers. These exist in most affected industries, and they mostly underdeliver, for reasons that are structural rather than cultural.

First, they run on attribution. A report in a meeting is a report with a name attached, so members share generalities and withhold specifics — and specifics are the intelligence. The route, the timing pattern, the method of defeating a particular control: these travel only when the teller cannot be identified as the victim.

Second, they run on goodwill, which is procyclical. Sharing is easiest when losses are low and least likely exactly when the sector is under coordinated pressure and the information matters most.

Third, they have no memory. A verbal warning at a quarterly meeting decays immediately. There is no cumulative record that a new pattern is the third occurrence of a known method rather than an isolated event, so the sector never converts incidents into trend.

## What attribution-protected intelligence looks like

Other domains solved this problem decades ago, and the pattern is consistent. Aviation safety runs on reporting systems where the reporter's identity is structurally protected, which is why near-miss data exists at all. Financial crime units exchange typologies — methods, patterns, indicators — rather than customer files. Cybersecurity built an entire discipline of indicator sharing in which what circulates is the signature of the attack, never the name of the victim.

The physical-loss version follows the same logic. What the sector needs from an incident is not who was hit. It is the pattern: method, geography at a useful resolution, time window, asset class targeted, and disposition — where the goods went. All of that can be extracted from an incident record and stripped of the details that identify the victim, then contributed to a shared picture that every member reads.

The mechanics matter. Contribution has to be structured — a defined incident schema, not free text — so that patterns aggregate instead of accumulating as anecdotes. De-identification has to be done at the source or by a neutral steward, not promised after the fact. And the shared picture has to be a living record with memory: a map of methods and corridors that gets sharper with each contribution, so members receive more value than they contribute. That asymmetry — read the whole sector, reveal nothing about yourself — is what makes participation rational for the first time.

This is a governance design before it is a technology design. The steward role is the hinge: someone has to hold the keys, enforce the schema, perform the de-identification, and be institutionally incapable of leaking attribution — because their standing in the sector depends on it. In practice that steward is rarely a member operator and rarely a government agency. It is a neutral party whose business is the intelligence itself.

## From shared picture to individual action

A sector-level picture changes what each member's own operational intelligence can do. A perimeter event that looks isolated on one farm or one depot reads differently against a record showing the same method used along the same corridor twice in the past quarter. Detection thresholds, patrol scheduling, and escalation rules can be conditioned on sector state rather than only local history — a real-time operational model in which the relevant context extends past the fence line.

The direction of travel matters here. The shared picture informs each member's decisions; it does not make them. Each operator retains its own controls, its own escalation rules, its own relationships with authorities. What the sector shares is the map, not the response — which is also what keeps participation compatible with competition. Members who compete on price and product do not compete on being robbed, and a well-designed intelligence pool touches only the second category.

There is a further, quieter benefit. Sectors that can show a disciplined, cumulative record of loss patterns hold a different conversation with insurers and authorities than sectors that offer anecdotes. Aggregate, de-identified evidence of an organized pattern is the difference between individual complaints and a documented sectoral condition — and documented conditions attract enforcement resources and fairer pricing in a way that individual reports never do.

## The operator is the hero; the steward is the guide

None of this asks operators to become more courageous about disclosure. That framing misreads the problem. Operators who withhold incident details are behaving rationally under an architecture that punishes speech with attribution. The fix is not braver members. It is an architecture in which the rational choice and the collectively useful choice are finally the same choice.

Building that architecture is guide's work: define the incident schema, establish the de-identification discipline, hold custody of the shared record, and return to every member a picture none of them could assemble alone. The sector already paid for the intelligence, incident by incident, year after year. The only question is whether it ever gets delivered.
